## 2. Premise: Atlantis, but modern

The sea folk draw on the human world's Atlantis, as told by Plato in the
*Critias* and *Timaeus*: a city of concentric rings around a central
palace, cut through by radial canals, rich in a red-gold metal called
**orichalcum**, with hot and cold springs and great harbours. Archaeology has
long linked the legend to Bronze Age Crete and Thera, so the Minoan world
supplies a second, older layer: columns that taper downward, light wells,
spiral bands and frescoes of dolphins and octopus.

Like the lava people, the sea folk are scientifically advanced. Their
architecture is modern rather than ruined or ancient: continuous nacre shells,
orichalcum structure, large curved glass, and pressurised **air halls** in
which they walk on legs.

They are a people, not gods, and nothing in their city is a temple.

## 2a. Two eras, and a city in the water column (third pass, proposed)

The first plans built everything on the seabed, as a land people would. The sea
folk swim in three dimensions; nothing ties them to the floor except habit and
history. The city now shows both: an old city on the floor and a new one
risen into the water above it.

Under both lies a third layer, older than anything the classical city kept:
**the first city**. Its spires still stand here and there inside the walls,
some whole and some broken off, never restored and never taken down. Its sea
wall is the dark lower course of the city walls (section 4a).

### The classical city: the floor

What the sea folk built before air halls: **Tidehall**, the **Ring Current**, the
eight sunken **avenues**, the **school court**, the **Lamp House** and the
**Current House**'s old family homes beside the current. It is preserved, not
ruined: kept, cleaned and still used, the city's ceremonial ground.

- **Grounded and heavy.** Masonry-like nacre shells on socketed plinths,
  symmetrical, on the avenues' axes. Built up from the seabed, as land
  architecture is.
- **Atlantean and Minoan.** Plato's concentric rings and radial canals; Minoan
  columns tapering downward with cushion capitals, light wells, spiral and wave
  bands, frescoes of dolphins and octopus; orichalcum as *plating* on the ring
  walls, as Plato describes, never as structure.
- **SuperEggs at the square end.** Epsilon about 4.5 to 5.5: blocky, softened
  masses that read as carved and stacked. Openings are tall superellipse arches
  (exponent about 4).
- **Lit from within.** At 72 m almost no daylight arrives. The floor lives by
  living pearl lamps, which is why the Lamp House is one of its oldest crafts.

### The modern city: glass and bubbles

What the sea folk build now, since the Glassworks learned to make sealed glass.
Two kinds of work, both in SuperEggs (revised 2026-10-13 after review):

- **On the floor, it keeps the heritage.** New glass is brought to the old
  buildings in three ways:
  - **Wrap**, after the British Museum's Great Court. A glass bubble is set over a
    preserved classical building, which stands free inside it, now dry. The
    Exchange wraps the old **Hall of Records**, the city archive.
  - **Join**, after the Singapore National Gallery. Two preserved buildings are
    each wrapped close in glass and linked by a new glass room. The guest hall
    joins the two **Strangers' Houses**, where the classical city lodged
    visitors, with a glass common room that bridges the avenue between them.
  - **Graft.** New glass is carried on old masonry. The floor-level glass
    tunnels lie on the classical ring arcade, its bridges over the Ring
    Current and the avenue colonnade, so swimmers still pass beneath through
    the arches. The four glass trunks rise from the stumps of the old ring
    towers.
- **In the column, it is whole bubbles with floors.** The modern city is:
  - complete SuperEgg bubbles in the open water, each with a floor, held steady
    by a nacre ballast keel beneath it;
  - four vertical glass **trunks** with spiral ramps, each threading a
    household's **landing hall**, with the family's wet homes docked round it;
  - a **crown ring** of glass tunnel joining the trunks' tops 30 m up, with a
    **crown hall** where it meets each trunk.

  Nothing hangs on cables, stands on legs or wears a cage of ribs.
- **Why up.** Light and room. Higher water is brighter and warmer, and the
  modern households live 16 to 20 m above the floor. Kaluja's "Sunlight reaches
  us in ribbons" is literal: the higher halls catch it.
- **SuperEggs at the round end.** Epsilon about 2 to 2.6 for bubbles and homes.
  The glass is frameless and curved, with a few broad nacre bands at floor
  lines and a nacre keel below. Arrangement is rings and spokes, not stalks.

### How the two eras map onto each other

1. **Plato's rings, built twice.** On the floor, the civic ring tunnel runs on
   the old arcade round the castle, with four spokes over the Ring Current to
   the trunks, and the avenue tunnel runs out to the Glassworks. Thirty metres
   up, the crown ring joins the four trunks. The modern network repeats the
   classical plan in three dimensions.
2. **The old is never altered.** New glass stands at least 1.5 m clear of a
   heritage wall and touches old work only at a nacre collar that could be
   taken off again. Old and new stay easy to tell apart.
3. **Height is age.** The floor is the oldest; the higher the structure, the
   newer. A visitor sinking from the surface passes from the newest city to the
   oldest.
4. **Tidehall under glass: the civic hall.** The palace is kept whole inside
   one glass SuperEgg 44 m across and 32 m high, and is dry. The city's
   business is done there: audiences in the old hall, the assembly on the
   palace roof terrace under the crown of the glass, hearings round its feet.
   Over the mound nothing else modern stands but the central trunk, which
   drops into the civic hall from the fountain's shaft. Daylight falls down
   the column through the fountain's glass floor.
5. **The Glassworks is the hinge.** It is the first glass bubble, socketed by
   the warm vent that heats its furnace, and is where the new era began. The
   avenue tunnel joins it to the network.
6. **Enclosed.** The modern city closed itself in. It raised the first city's
   sea wall above the water in a lighter stone and roofed the whole city
   (section 4a). It built them in the classical manner, so from outside the city
   reads as one old fortress.

### Shared between the eras

- **Materials:** nacre and orichalcum.
- **Signature band:** the spiral and wave band. On the floor it is carved at a
  shell's springing line; on glass it is the broad nacre band at the floor line.
- **Light:** pearl lamps.
- **Design language:** SuperEggs and superellipse openings throughout. The eras
  differ in epsilon, proportion and arrangement, never in vocabulary.

### Physics the plan respects

**The air (proposed, 2026-10-14).** The second rule below replaces "pool locks
everywhere": the plan's check found that one pressure cannot hold open pools
at many depths.

- **One sealed network at one pressure.** Every dry space you can walk in (the
  trunks, the tunnels, the halls, the wraps) is one body of air, so walkers go
  up and down the trunks' spiral ramps and along the tunnels with no door
  anywhere inside it. That is what the trunks are for: inside each is dry air
  and a ramp, 1.4 m wide, in half-turn flights of 32 degrees at most with a
  landing between flights and at every floor.
- **The datum sets the pressure.** An open pool keeps its air only where the
  air's pressure equals the sea's at the pool's surface, and the sea's pressure
  rises by a tenth of a bar for every metre down. So the network has one depth
  where open pools work: its **datum**, the walking floor at its foot, 4.4 m
  above the city floor and 67.6 m down. The civic ring, the spokes, the trunk
  feet, the guest hall's common room and the Glassworks' floor are all at the
  datum. The network's air is at the sea's pressure there, about 7.85 bar.
- **Moon pools at the datum.** Every open entry is a **moon pool** in a floor
  at the datum (within 0.3 m): a well about 2 m deep with a nacre rim, its
  water standing 0.4 m below the floor. A swimmer rises into it and steps out;
  there is no door. There are eleven: the guest hall's common room (opening
  down into the avenue), the Glassworks (its well runs down through the socket
  and out onto the main avenue), one at each trunk foot (the old ring tower's
  chamber, flooded, is its well, and the old tower door its mouth), four in the
  civic hall and one in the Exchange. The last five are in halls whose floors
  are below the datum (the civic hall's old terrace 2.4 m below, the trading
  floor 3.8 m below), where an open pool would flood the floor: each stands on
  a nacre drum or dais whose rim is at the datum, with steps down to the floor,
  and its well leaves through the glass by a short duct.
- **Lock chambers above the datum.** Higher up, the sea's pressure is lower than
  the network's (by 1.1 to 1.6 bar at the landing halls, 2.6 bar at the crown
  halls), and an open pool would blow the air out. Every way in up there is a
  **lock chamber**: a small chamber of nacre and glass with two round hatches
  in orichalcum rims. Each landing and crown hall has a sea lock hung under its
  floor on the city side, clear of the keel round the trunk, with its inner
  hatch in the floor and its outer hatch opening down to the sea. A swimmer
  enters the flooded chamber, the outer hatch closes, and the network's own
  overpressure blows the water out through a valve in the chamber's floor, so
  no pump is needed; then the inner hatch opens. Leaving reverses it: the
  chamber's air is let out to the sea and the sea floods in. Each wet home docks
  to its landing hall through a short level lock in the docking collar, worked
  the same way. There are twenty-one lock chambers: eight sea locks, twelve
  home locks and the fountain lock. No entry lies below the datum, where a lock
  would need a pump.
- **The fountain lock.** The central trunk's top meets the air under the roof,
  at the surface's pressure, 6.8 bar below the network's. A lock chamber in the
  trunk's top, just under the waterline landing, joins them. It is the one
  slow lock in the city: its air is let up or down through a small valve while
  whoever is inside waits.
- **The air supply.** Damaku's air engine in the Glassworks, its bellows worked
  by the warm vent's heat, draws air from the roof's air hall down a stone air
  shaft built inside the wall over the main gate and through a conduit in the
  rock to the Glassworks, and presses it into the network. Spent air leaves the
  only way it can: it spills out under the rims of the moon pools, which are
  the network's relief valves, so the pressure can never rise above the
  datum's. The air each lock cycle spends is made up the same way. The
  Glassworks was the first air bubble, and its floor set the datum that every
  later dry space keeps.
- **The glass.** Above the datum the network's air pushes outward on the glass
  by about 0.1 bar for every metre of height: the landing and crown halls'
  glass is in tension, held by its nacre bands, and the top of the central
  trunk carries the most. Below the datum (the trading floor, the guest
  houses' floors, the civic hall's terrace) the sea presses in a little and the
  floors are sealed.
- **Air is buoyant,** so every bubble in the water carries a nacre ballast keel
  that holds it level and still, and the trunks and tunnels brace one another.
  Nothing is held down by cables, and nothing stands on legs.
- **Homes are wet,** docked to their landing hall with openings at least 3 m
  clear; sea folk swim in from outside, and meet visitors on legs in the hall.
- **The hero walks or swims.** The whole network is walkable without a Diving
  Helmet, from the guest hall to every household's landing and the lookout.
  Everything else is reached by swimming, as the sea folk reach it.

### Air halls and the change to legs

Some work cannot be done underwater: glass is blown in air, paper and charts
keep only when dry, and air-breathing visitors need somewhere to stand. The sea
folk build **air halls**, sealed glass bubbles filled with air.

- **Entry.** An air hall on the city floor is entered from below through an
  open moon pool at the network's datum: a swimmer rises through the pool and
  climbs out onto the floor by a broad step, with no door to open. A hall
  higher in the column is entered through a lock chamber under its floor, whose
  outer hatch closes behind the swimmer before the hall's own air blows the
  water out (section 2a, "Physics the plan respects").
- **Tail to legs.** As a sea person climbs out of the pool, their tail parts
  into legs, and they walk the hall on foot. Re-entering the water, the legs
  close back into a tail. The change is part of their nature, not a magic
  item.
- **For the hero.** An air hall is the one place in the city where the hero
  can breathe and walk without a Diving Helmet, and talk to the sea folk face
  to face on dry ground.

### Governance

The city's business is done in the civic hall, the palace kept dry under glass:
audiences in the old hall, the assembly on the palace roof terrace.

The Tidekeeper rules, but as a listener and judge. Each household speaks for
itself in the audience hall. The hero fights the Tidekeeper because fighting to
test a visitor's mettle is part of sea folk culture: a challenge of respect,
not hostility.

### Isolation

The sea folk are protective, and by choice isolationist. They walled and
roofed their city, and they keep its four sea gates shut. The city's own come
and go by a small swim-wicket in each gate. To ships and swimmers the city is a
stone fortress standing out of the sea, with a fountain on its roof and no door
at the waterline. The way in that the city does not advertise runs through the
fountain. The luminous trail across the seabed still leads to the main gate; it
is older than the walls. This sharpens the discord below: a people already
inclined to close their gates find every reason to keep them closed.

### Relations: discord now, harmony later

When the hero arrives, every community in the kingdom is at odds with the
others. The Demon King has sown the discord; how is still open. Restoring
harmony between them is the kingdom's story, as it is in the Sky Kingdom.

- **The island village:** the shoreline exchange, generations old, has broken
  off. Each side blames the other for damage to its waters. Pelaju and the
  island's elders, who were friends, no longer speak; Talisa's work with the
  island's marine biologist has stopped.
- **The pirates:** heard overhead "like distant wooden thunder." The sea folk
  hate both crews and believe the ships are fouling the reef.
- **The Kraken and Fish Goblins:** a danger Ikara and Tudaro watch. The goblins
  serve the Kraken, not the city.

## 4. Settlement structure

Third pass (section 2a; floor plans in `ocean_sea_folk_city_layout.md` 10). On
the floor:

6. **Radial avenues:** eight sunken avenues lit by luminous stones.
7. **The vent edge:** the Glassworks, the first glass bubble, at the head of

In the column:

  - the Garden House;
  - the Glassworks household, whose trunk climbs on to the Kraken lookout;
  - the Exchange household;
  - the Reef House, with its workshop.

### The guest hall: the city's rest point

The guest hall is an air hall kept by **Isaro**. It replaces the existing
`seafolk_village_inn` (whose keeper, Nerissa Stillwater, is Isaro under her new
name) at nearly the same place, and is a rest point, one of two in the kingdom
(the other is Kai Mālie's guest house). Resting restores the party and melted blorbs,
advances to morning and plays the standard waking sequence in a dry bed inside
the hall; the party gathers on the hall's floor, never in the moon pool. A
night costs 25 Tokoins, the existing inn's price and the kingdom's rate,
through the shared transaction interface.

### Glass tunnels

Sealed **glass tunnels** join every dry space into one network, so sea folk on
legs and an unhelmeted hero can walk the city without entering the water.

- **Form:** a superellipse tube of frameless curved glass, its floor flat, with
  a broad nacre collar every 12.8 m. On the floor it lies on the old arcades
  and bridges; in the column it is braced between the trunks.
- **Routes:**
  - the civic ring round the castle, 4.4 m up, threading the Exchange and the
    guest hall;
  - four spokes over the Ring Current to the trunk feet;
  - the avenue tunnel to the Glassworks;
  - the trunks' spiral ramps;
  - the crown ring, 30 m up.
- **Swimmers** pass under the floor-level tunnels through the arcade's
  arches, with 3.75 m clear.
- **Dimensions:** walking floor at least 3 m wide, headroom at least 3.2 m,
  1:6 or gentler; trunk ramps 1.4 m wide at 32° with landings.
- **Air:** the whole network is one sealed volume at one pressure, as the
  spaceship's cabin and docking neck are, so no tunnel or trunk has a door.
  Its pressure is the sea's at the datum, the tunnels' 4.4 m floor; swimmers
  enter by the moon pools at that level and by lock chambers above it.

## 4a. The walls and the roof (proposed, 2026-10-13)

- **Plan:** an octagon that reads as a square with short mitred corners. The
  inner faces of the sides are 135 m from the centre, so each side is 199 m and
  each mitre 50 m. The walls are 8 m thick. The four avenues at 0, 90, 180 and
  270 degrees meet the middles of the sides; the diagonal avenues meet the
  mitres.
- **Height:** from the seabed, 72 m down, to a flat roof 24 m above the sea,
  about a third again above the water.
- **Stone:** to 2.5 m above the waterline, the first city's sea wall, a dark,
  sturdy stone in heavy rusticated courses. Above it, a lighter stone with
  pilasters every 20 m, string courses, a cornice and a parapet. The string
  courses and rustication give a climber holds.
- **Inside:** the sea up to the waterline, then 16 m of air under a coffered
  vault. Eight piers at 100 m and four at 60 m carry the vault, in the dark
  stone below the water and the light stone above it. Lamps hang over the
  water.
- **Light:** eight glass-capped wells in the roof at 60 m over the avenues, and
  a glass ring in the floor of the fountain's lowest basin over the open
  column.
- **Gates:** four sea gates, 14 m wide and 24 m tall, on the seabed where the
  avenues meet the sides. They are shut, and each leaf has a swim-wicket.
- **The roof:** a stone plaza. At its centre is a four-tiered working fountain,
  its basins 40, 26, 14 and 6 m across, with a jet at the top. Behind the curtain
  from the second tier a pivoting stone in the pedestal opens on a stone stair.
  The stair runs down a shaft through the roof to a landing at the waterline,
  where the glass central trunk takes over and descends into the civic hall.
- **The Kraken lookout:** a glass watch room on the parapet over the main gate,
  facing the islands. It replaces the lookout bubble on the 112.5 trunk.

## 5. Architectural charter (third pass, proposed)

Two eras, each a closed kit (section 2a), over the remnants of a third.

| | **Classical: the heritage** | **Modern: the glass** |
|---|---|---|
| Share of the city | about 45 percent of the built mass | about 55 percent |
| Sources | Atlantean (Plato's rings and canals) with Minoan named elements | invented; the heritage moves follow the British Museum's Great Court and the Singapore National Gallery |
| Massing | grounded, symmetrical, on axes; shells on socketed nacre plinths; arcades, bridges and ring towers | whole bubbles with floors; wraps round heritage; tunnels in rings and spokes; trunks |
| SuperEgg epsilon | 4.5 to 5.5 (carved, stacked) | 2.0 to 2.6 (bubbles, homes, wraps) |
| Openings | tall superellipse arches, exponent about 4 | the glass itself; open moon pools at the datum, round-hatched lock chambers above it; swim openings at least 3 m clear in homes |
| Structure | the shell itself; Minoan columns tapering downward, with cushion capitals, red or ocean blue | frameless curved glass; broad nacre bands at floor lines; a nacre ballast keel under each bubble in the water |
| Orichalcum | plating on the ring walls and the band | fittings only: rails, counters, hatch rims |
| Ornament | spiral and wave bands carved at the springing line; frescoes of dolphins and octopus | the band as a broad nacre ring at the floor line; nothing else |
| Palette | nacre white and teal; prism green crowns; column red and blue | clear and faintly green glass, nacre, warm lamp light inside |

- **Shared invariants:** nacre and orichalcum; the spiral and wave band on every
  building of both eras; pearl lamps (the accent, about 5 percent); SuperEggs
  and superellipses throughout.
- **Hierarchy:**
  - The civic hall, with Tidehall inside it, is the polite building of the
    city.
  - Tidehall is the polite building of the classical city.
  - The Exchange is the polite building of the modern one.
  - The Glassworks is the hinge between the eras.
- **Heritage rules:** new glass stands 1.5 m clear of old walls, meets old work
  only at a removable nacre collar, and never mixes its massing with the old.
- **Exclusions:**
  - from other traditions: Greek temple orders and pediments (they belong to
    the Sky Kingdom); Minoan horns of consecration, double axes and other
    sacred emblems;
  - from the wrong mood: picturesque ruins and scattered broken columns (the
    first city's spires are the one exception: real remnants kept where they
    stand, never restored, never added to, and nothing new touching them);
    anything that reads as a
    sunken human city; boxes;
  - from earlier passes: light pipes; cables and stalks; anything standing on
    legs or stilts; bird-cage ribs or fine metal grids on glass; columns in the
    column.

## 5a. Dress charter

Written to the `character-design` skill. The prototype's choices stand: teal
skin, shiny scale clothing (silver formal, dark blue, brown for work),
sleeveless or short sleeves, some cropped tops, and shells or sea flowers in

## Implementation notes

- **Air halls reuse the existing pressurised-volume idea.** The spaceship
  already registers in the `pressurized_volumes` group and answers
  `contains_breathable_point()`, which `AtmosphereLayer` consults. An air hall
  needs the same kind of query from the liquid system: inside the hall's air,
  a body is out of the water, stops swimming, breathes and walks. The moon pool
  surface is an ordinary water surface with swim exits. A lock chamber is a
  small dry volume of its own that floods and empties between its two hatches. The same shared
  **dry volume** system the pirate ships need below their waterline does this
  (`ocean_pirate_ships_design.md`, section 6): no liquid inside, and no
  underwater fog or tint while the camera is inside.
- **New sea folk tail (user direction).** Replace the segmented `AquaticTail`
  on the sea folk with the same design as the player's mermaid tail
  (`blorb_suit.gd`, `MERMAID_TAIL_*`, `build_mermaid_tail_mesh()`, worn when two
  Water leg blorbs swim under the Nautilus Crown), but sized differently: it
  starts at the width of the hips rather than wide enough to enclose two legs,
  and emerges from the hips seamlessly. The hips use the tail's colour and
  material.
- **Legged form.** When a sea person shifts to legs in an air hall, the full
  hips, leg segments and feet are the exact same colour and material as their
  tail. The rig needs a legged variant and a transition at the pool edge. Use
  the `figure-rig` skill.
- **Fish Goblins keep the segmented tail.** Its lobster-like look is
  deliberately unsettling and stays theirs alone.
