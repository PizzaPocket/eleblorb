Moy.

A dense, walkable city standing up out of the desert on the west side of the Crossroads. It has no cars, no trains, no roads out and no water nearby. Four giant turbines power it, and it grows its own food.

400 m across, Rim Walk to Rim Walk 45 named residents 280 m Windspire 4 turbines, 160 m to the tip +6.5 m the Deck

Where Moy stands

Moy sits on the gorge floor, 60 m below the main plateau, in a desert basin ringed by the main plateau's cliff, the Jungle and Ice plateaus, and the world's edge. The nearest open water is the eastern lake, about 760 m away and 60 m up.

North is up (−Z in the game). The turbines stay on the city's side of the Crossroads.

Every building

Each rectangle is one building, shoulder to shoulder along the streets: homes over shops in Old Moy and the market lanes, podiums and shops round the estate towers, office podiums downtown. The numbered buildings are the planned ones, listed below with who lives or works in each. Every street is named and listed below. In the street blocks every building fronts a street, lane or square, and the only ground inside a block is a shared garden courtyard. Downtown is filled with mid-rise blocks of 10 to 22 storeys round the towers. The North Estate is two precincts: broad blocks close together in a ring round each precinct's court, joined by covered linkways (dashed). Green marks gardens and the park only.

Streets

Every street joins another street, a square, the Park Ring or the Rim Walk at both ends. There are no dead ends.

The park and the giant playground

A four-part garden at the centre of the city, after the Persian chahar bagh: a dry garden of desert plants, a reed pond that cleans the city's reclaimed water, Old Fu's tea house and chess terrace under timber parasols (after Seville's Metropol Parasol), and one irrigated great lawn. Four steel wind trees, after the Arbre à Vent and Singapore's Supertrees, light it at night with their leaf turbines. The playground sits where the civic axis and the north–south walk cross.

The playground is one sculpted desert landscape, after Monstrum's climbing worlds, the knitted Woods of Net in Hakone and Toa Payoh's mosaic dragon. Children reach every part by nets, ramps and climbing walls. The hero gets a route for each power, sized to his real reach: 1.2 m steps for an ordinary jump, perches at 12 m for a blorb super-jump, the wind trees as Leaf Hat anchors, a 14 m cloud gap for the Air suit, hover gates over the splash pad, eight hoops for the crystal skates' ribbon, and the pump track for the dirt bike.

Downtown's towers

Each tower answers the desert sun, the west wind and the city's history in its own way. The Windspire twists a quarter-turn as it rises, after the Shanghai Tower, and turbines spin through three wind gaps, after Guangzhou's Pearl River Tower. The glass tower wears a moving screen, after Abu Dhabi's Al Bahar Towers, pierced with Old Moy's painted house marks; it closes against the sun and opens at dusk. The north tower is plug-in start-up capsules on a concrete core, after Tokyo's Nakagin Capsule Tower, with an open sky garden halfway up. The south tower's balconies ripple like dunes, after Chicago's Aqua Tower. The Grand Hotel is the old one, the boom's idea of the future, after John Portman: a white ribbed cylinder with glass lifts climbing its face to a revolving drum, after Atlanta's Westin Peachtree Plaza and the Westin Bonaventure in Los Angeles.

True scale, side by side. The capsules, balconies, screens and fins are also the city's best climbing holds and swing anchors.

The Grand Hotel

The city's first tall building and its living room, redesigned in John Portman's manner (proposed, 2026-10-13). With almost no outside visitors, it lives on Moy itself: tea and weddings under the chandelier, dinner in the Chandelier Room, a drink in the Windrose at the top, five floors of long-stay residences, and guest rooms for the rare traveller like the hero. The Court, a block of twelve levels, wraps an atrium 44 m high, after Portman's Hyatt Regency in Atlanta: rooms open off planted galleries round the void, and the south galleries lean in storey by storey, after his Hyatt Regency in San Francisco. A dry rock garden and pavilion stand on the atrium floor, after Guangzhou's White Swan; a double ramp rises to the mezzanine, where a skybridge brings in the Deck; the chandelier's three thousand crystals hang down the middle of the void. A white ribbed cylinder rises on the north, four glass lifts climbing its face from the atrium floor, through the skylight, to the revolving Windrose in the drum, which hides the water tanks. Two wind towers on the roof drop the west wind into the atrium, and the city's reclaimed water crosses its floor in a runnel on its way to the park's reed pond: working water, not a fountain. No drive for cars: a shaded forecourt faces the plaza. Lucinda keeps the desk herself in the evenings, and the party's suite is on level 27, facing the park.

City hall

The third of the boom's civic buildings, at the head of Civic Plaza, facing the library down the civic axis across the park. The hero never goes inside: city hall is a landmark and a climb, built as an exterior. Low interlocking stone masses and one clock tower, after Willem Dudok's Hilversum Town Hall. The wings step down either side at 14 m with long bronze window bands; the front block rises to 22 m over a portico of six square piers and three pairs of bronze doors cast with house marks, which stay shut. The council chamber behind the five tall windows, walled in gold mosaic after Stockholm's Golden Hall, is only glimpsed as a gold glow at night, and its balcony over the plaza has a rail of house marks, where the city is addressed after the Spanish town hall. Behind it all, the 45 m clock tower on Clock Street closes the view up Meridian Avenue; its lit lantern is the plaza's highest swing anchor.

West front on Civic Plaza (left) and north side on Meridian North (right), true scale. Dotted: the climb, from the steps to the portico roof, the balcony, the parapet, then up the tower's stone bands to the clock, the belfry and the lantern. Lit windows are panels; no room is built behind them.

The North Estate

Public housing planned the way Singapore's Housing Board has since the late 1970s: not rows of blocks but precincts, each a ring of blocks round a shared court small enough for neighbours to know each other. The blocks are broad and close together, 10 to 20 m apart: slabs and broad point blocks with open corridors facing the court and the flats facing out, like Pinnacle@Duxton's linked blocks. The blocks that hold the street edges keep the city grid; behind them the blocks standing back in the gardens turn 30° together onto the estate's own lines, so the gaps meet the west wind on the diagonal and draw it through the courts, the long faces turn from the low western sun, and every flat looks out on a diagonal rather than down a street. In the west precinct the twins face the library, and the 10 m gap between them is the court's gate, spanned by their sky bridge and sky garden on the 26th floor. Each court has a playground, a chess pavilion and shade trees; a covered linkway runs round it from void deck to void deck; behind Block 9 the residents keep a community garden. The east precinct rings Estate Square with the neighbourhood centre and the hawker centre. The gaps are swing gaps: corridor rail to corridor rail with the Leaf Hat.

The Old Moy tower house

The kit for Old Moy, after the tower houses of old Sana'a: most stand on plumb ground storeys of dark stone, the oldest (about one in four, most of them round the first well) on a battered base of coursed rammed earth with rounded corners under a white gypsum cornice. Above either rise brick storeys banded at every floor in white gypsum friezes, every window outlined in white under a semicircular fanlight (the qamariya), box windows (the kishk) on brackets, and a crenellated parapet with whitewashed horns at the corners. The roof is a terrace: a kiosk where the ramp arrives, a wind tower turned to the west wind over it, and on some houses a rooftop room (the manzar). Inside, a switchback ramp core runs the full height and doubles as the house's air shaft. Each tower was built for one family; most are now a shop below and a flat on each floor above. Every Old Moy lot 6 m or more across takes the tower house; narrower lots hold the later tiled pencil infill.

The Reese–Miller house on Arcade Street, true scale: its street front (left) and who lives on each floor (right). A tailor's shop and three let flats below; Elizabeth and Dan on the top three floors, with the mafraj, the best room, at the top; Jammer's room is the manzar on the roof.

The market lanes

Moy's first growth outside the well town, after Hong Kong's tenement shophouses (tong lau): a shop at the ground and flats above, shoulder to shoulder. The boom houses, four and five storeys, were built for the Windworks' first workers: rendered fronts, French windows with shutters, and on the main streets a veranda on square columns over the pavement, stacked floor on floor. The tile houses, six to nine storeys, came as the city grew: mosaic tile, balconies, a canopy over the shop, and on corners the rounded Streamline corner with balconies wrapping it. Narrow pairs share one ramp core on their party wall, the way tong lau pairs share a stair; slivers too narrow for a house hold two-storey lock-up shops. Arcade Street is roofed in the manner of a Tokyo shōtengai, and the night market fills Lamp Lane under strings of lanterns.

The library

A classic building renovated over the years, after Brooklyn's Central Library. Built in the same boom as the Grand Hotel, in art deco limestone and bronze, its front curves round Library Square like an open book, with a 15 m portal at the spine framed in gilded bronze figures from Moy's stories. Inside is the Great Hall, after Baltimore's George Peabody Library and Trinity College's Long Room: reading tables with green lamps on the floor, four tiers of cast-iron galleries round every wall, every wall shelved, every shelf reachable from its gallery, all under a louvred skylight. Ada's renovation opened the ground floor in glass behind the old stone, moved the children's library round a garden court, joined the Deck, and turned the old basement stacks into the heritage room, where the Great Hall's creaking floorboards are relaid and Nia keeps her drawer of maps nobody can explain. The hero climbs the hall through its iron: rail to rail through the gaps where the rolling ladders hang.

Four layers of movement

  • 1 · GroundLanes 3.2 to 4.8 m, shopping streets 6.4 to 8 m, main streets 9.6 to 12.8 m. No walk goes round more than about 60 m.
  • 2 · The DeckA walkway at +6.5 m over the podiums and across the streets. Reached by ramp towers, glass lifts and travelators at about 12°.
  • 3 · TowersLifts in every lobby. Downtown has sky lobbies; the estate's twin blocks share a sky garden on the 26th floor.
  • 4 · RoofsRoofs step by at most one storey, with 2 to 4 m gaps across lanes, so the hero can cross the city roof to roof.

Streets of 6 to 13 m between walls of 16 to 40 m are swing corridors for the Leaf Hat: the rope runs 12 to 52 m, and an anchor must be at least 7 m up.
