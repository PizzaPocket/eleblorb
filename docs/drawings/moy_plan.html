Moy.

A dense, walkable city standing up out of the desert on the west side of the Crossroads. It has no cars, no trains, no roads out and no water nearby. Four giant turbines power it, and it grows its own food.

400 m across, Rim Walk to Rim Walk 45 named residents 280 m Windspire 4 turbines, 160 m to the tip +6.5 m the Deck

Where Moy stands

Moy sits on the gorge floor, 60 m below the main plateau, in a desert basin ringed by the main plateau's cliff, the Jungle and Ice plateaus, and the world's edge. The nearest open water is the eastern lake, about 760 m away and 60 m up.

North is up (−Z in the game). The turbines stay on the city's side of the Crossroads.

Every building

Each rectangle is one building, shoulder to shoulder along the streets: homes over shops in Old Moy and the market lanes, podiums and shops round the estate towers, office podiums downtown. The numbered buildings are the planned ones, listed below with who lives or works in each. Every street is named and listed below. In the street blocks every building fronts a street, lane or square, and the only ground inside a block is a shared garden courtyard. Downtown is filled with mid-rise blocks of 18 to 34 storeys round the towers, each tower standing back on its own public ground. The North Estate is two precincts: broad blocks close together in a ring round each precinct's court, joined by covered linkways (dashed). Green marks gardens and the park only.

Streets

Every street joins another street, a square, the Park Ring or the Rim Walk at both ends. There are no dead ends.

The park, the Crossing and the giant playground

A shared park at the centre of the city. At its middle, where the cross walks meet and the two lines cross, stands the Crossing, the city's station (revised 2026-10-17, plan iteration 3, designed from what people do there). Every change of line is between one Spine platform (+14.2) and one high-line platform (+37), so there are four garden towers, one in each quarter, in the corner where a Spine platform and a high-line platform pass each other: each a steel trunk with a double-helix escalator wound round it, galleries at the concourse and both platforms a short bridge from each, and a glass lift on its inner corner opening straight onto both platforms. Any change of line is one tower, up or down. The towers are the structure: head beams between them carry the Spine and the high line (no other column stands under either line here), the cross-bridges hang from the Spine's, and one louvred roof rests on their tops at +41, over the high line's platforms. Climbers cover the mesh between each tower's ribs, after Singapore's Supertrees and WOHA's Oasia. The concourse at the Deck's level is the Crossing Ring, a garden walk round the towers that the Deck's Spine walks join, with two cross-bridges under the Spine's tracks joining the towers and both Spine platforms; the middle is open from the lawn to the sky, and a rain garden rings the towers' feet, fed by the roof. Round it, four quarters, after the Persian chahar bagh: a dry garden of rocks, agave and date palms; a reed pond that cleans the city's reclaimed water, under a banyan; Old Fu's tea house and chess terrace under flame trees and timber parasols; and the great lawn. Ghafs, the desert's shade trees, ring the pond and the playground, and date palms line the cross walk under the high line.

The giant playground now has the south-east quarter to itself, the whole landscape moved from the centre at a little over half its old size, beside the green. The playground is one sculpted desert landscape, after Monstrum's climbing worlds, the knitted Woods of Net in Hakone and Toa Payoh's mosaic dragon. Children reach every part by nets, ramps and climbing walls. The hero gets a route for each power, sized to his real reach: 1.2 m steps for an ordinary jump, perches at 12 m for a blorb super-jump, the Crossing's towers and the park's tall trees as Leaf Hat anchors, a 14 m cloud gap for the Air suit, hover gates over the splash pad, eight hoops for the crystal skates' ribbon, and the pump track for the dirt bike.

Old Moy

The well town, older than the city: tall, narrow tower houses of 4 to 9 storeys with lime-plastered walls, roof terraces and rooftop wind towers, round the first well on Cinema Square. Its painted house marks were the first picture signs. Its oldest sections keep the well town's own lanes: the Old High Street, the caravan road that bends north-east from Cinema Square and the first well, its southern stretch cut off by Clinic Lane, and Mara's Lane, a short old branch from it. The boom's grid was laid round and across them, so its streets meet the old roads at odd angles, as Manhattan's grid meets Broadway and Greenwich Village. Every block is a perimeter of tower houses facing its roads, with shared courtyards in the middle, after Dubai's Al Fahidi quarter.

Downtown's towers

Each tower answers the desert sun, the west wind and the city's history in its own way. The Windspire twists a quarter-turn as it rises, after the Shanghai Tower, and turbines spin through three wind gaps, after Guangzhou's Pearl River Tower. The BLOCK tower, on the plaza's south side, is a windowless dark shaft after New York's 33 Thomas Street, vent hoods where windows should be, under a crown of its own: above 150 m its four faces fold inward to a knife-edge ridge set close to the plaza face and canted, high at the west end, so from Civic Plaza it is a narrowing dark shard and from the side a blade raised toward the plaza, all inside its own footprint. Its one light is a single vertical azure slit in the crown's plaza face, like a slit pupil. The north tower is plug-in start-up capsules on a concrete core, after Tokyo's Nakagin Capsule Tower, with an open sky garden halfway up. The south tower's balconies ripple like dunes, after Chicago's Aqua Tower. The Grand Hotel is the old one, the boom's idea of the future, after John Portman: a white ribbed cylinder with glass lifts climbing its face to a revolving drum, after Atlanta's Westin Peachtree Plaza and the Westin Bonaventure in Los Angeles.

No tower takes its whole plot: each shaft stands at least 6 m back from its neighbours, on public ground with a life of its own (the Windspire's wind garden, the makers' yard under the capsules, the south tower's colonnade and residents' garden). The Windspire's wind gaps are slots between a broad core and three mega-columns that run straight through them, not a waist. BLOCK is the only dark thing downtown, and everything sinister in Moy is concentrated there. Its tower faces Civic Plaza across Meridian South over a bare forecourt of hot paving and raked black gravel, with one door; nobody the hero knows works inside except its director, Dr. Kask, who lives on the top floors. Behind it, BLOCK's first labs: a windowless base set back so the floors above lean over the pavement, slit windows, one door off its axis, a court seen only as an azure glow at night, and a ring of grounding masts. Precedents: 33 Thomas Street, New York, and Boston City Hall.

The Meridian Arch is the East Gate (revised 2026-10-16). It replaces the mid-rise blocks that once stood there, with Civic Plaza open in front of it (revised 2026-10-17: the arch is now City Hall). A city-owned loop after Beijing's CCTV headquarters whose two legs straddle the wall either side of Meridian Avenue, the avenue passing under it through a 24 m opening. It is one station on three levels: a hall in each leg opening on the court; the Wall Line's East Gate station at +12 m, its tracks and platform running through a train hall cut through both legs (dashed); and the high line's terminus on the lower bridge (+32 to +42 m), its platforms directly over the Wall Line at right angles. On each leg's court face an open cascade of 30° escalating ramps climbs the diagrid under glass canopies, in flights of about 6 m, after the Centre Pompidou and Singapore's open-air escalators, glass lifts beside it. The top bridge (+58 to +72 m) cantilevers 12 m toward city hall and holds the Wall Watch's command room. From the desert it frames the Timekeeper, as the St. Louis arch frames its city. The West Gate does the same more plainly: two towers straddling the wall either side of West Street, the Wall Line through them, the high line's west station on the bridge between them at +32 to +42 m, and a siding for its trains beyond. The six-storey building that was BLOCK's labs is ordinary mid-rise real estate now.

True scale, side by side. The capsules, balconies, screens and fins are also the city's best climbing holds and swing anchors.

The Grand Hotel

The city's first tall building and its living room, redesigned in John Portman's manner (proposed, 2026-10-13). With almost no outside visitors, it lives on Moy itself: tea and weddings under the chandelier, dinner in the Chandelier Room, a drink in the Windrose at the top, five floors of long-stay residences, and guest rooms for the rare traveller like the hero. The Court, a block of twelve levels, wraps an atrium 44 m high, after Portman's Hyatt Regency in Atlanta: rooms open off planted galleries round the void, and the south galleries lean in storey by storey, after his Hyatt Regency in San Francisco. A dry rock garden and pavilion stand on the atrium floor, after Guangzhou's White Swan; a double ramp rises to the mezzanine, where a skybridge brings in the Deck; the chandelier's three thousand crystals hang down the middle of the void. A white ribbed cylinder rises on the north, four glass lifts riding in slots let into its face from the atrium floor to the revolving Windrose in the drum, which hides the water tanks. The skylight is one pitch falling away from the tower to a gutter on the front, and the chandelier hangs from an arm cantilevered from the tower's core. Two wind towers on the roof drop the west wind into the atrium, and the city's reclaimed water crosses its floor in a runnel on its way to the park's reed pond: working water, not a fountain. No drive for cars: a shaded forecourt faces the plaza. Lucinda keeps the desk herself in the evenings, and the party's suite is on level 27, facing the park.

City Hall and the clock

City Hall is the Meridian Arch (revised 2026-10-17, at the user's direction). The old city hall at the head of the civic axis is gone, and the Timekeeper with it. City Hall's public hall opens at the foot of the arch's legs, beside the station hall; the city's offices fill the legs; the council chamber and the mayor's office sit in the top bridge's cantilever, looking down the civic axis over the new Civic Plaza (the old city hall's site, Clock Street and Meridian Court, one square in front of the arch) to the park; the Wall Watch keeps its command room in the top bridge's east end. Civic Plaza, the grand square (revised again 2026-10-17, at the user's direction): the commercial blocks that briefly stood on the old plaza hid the Grand Hotel's front, so they, Meridian Walk and Hotel Passage are gone. Civic Plaza now runs the whole way from the park to City Hall, 124 by 60 m, after Washington's Mall and Tiananmen Square: a long water mirror down the axis under the high line, its tree piers standing in the water; lawns either side, crossed on the hotel's door by a walk with a fountain in each lawn; an open paved forecourt before City Hall. Monument and statue plinths mark sites whose subjects are open. City Hall's front is new too: the Rostrum, a balcony at the Wall Line's level across the whole front and over the gate; the City Steps before each leg, a broad stair between an up and a down escalator; and on each leg a glazed scissor of escalator pairs up to the high line and the sky garden.

The city's clock is on the Crossing, at the middle of the city where the most people pass: a glass dome on the Crossing's roof, its drum a rounded square in plan (the roof's own shape), with a ring clock 16 m across set flush in each of its four sides, facing north, south, east and west, rising to about 70 m. Drum and cupola are one glass shell on a fine triangulated grid of slender white members, after the Great Court of the British Museum: no posts and no ring beam, every pane a triangle. Each clock is a ring clock: the hour ring inside the minute ring, each with a lit arrow, minute ticks on a fixed bezel; the seconds bead is gone. Its hollow centre is an open eye through the glass, lined in bronze, with a sill: a way in for climbers, gliders and fliers. A great bronze bell hangs from the crown ring: it rings at dawn (5:00) and half an hour before sunset (19:30) in the Angelus pattern (three strokes three times, then nine), and twelve strokes at noon.

The Clock Room is the dome's floor, a public room under the glass and the bell, reached on purpose: the garden towers' escalators end at the high line, so everyone gets off at the station first. Its ways in are its designed openings, all drawn on the plan. At the middle of each high-line platform, under the clock, the platform widens into a landing, and two escalator pairs climb from it through glass-guarded wells into the room. The four tower lifts run on to glass lanterns in the room, their doors facing the bell. The four eyes are open to the sky. A bronze compass rose lies under the bell. The clocks' eyes are for fliers and gliders only: nothing rises to them.

The North Estate

Public housing planned the way Singapore's Housing Board has since the late 1970s: not rows of blocks but precincts, each a ring of blocks round a shared court small enough for neighbours to know each other. The blocks are broad and close together, 10 to 20 m apart: slabs and broad point blocks with open corridors facing the court and the flats facing out, like Pinnacle@Duxton's linked blocks. The blocks that hold the street edges keep the city grid; behind them the blocks standing back in the gardens turn 30° together onto the estate's own lines, so the gaps meet the west wind on the diagonal and draw it through the courts, the long faces turn from the low western sun, and every flat looks out on a diagonal rather than down a street. In the west precinct the twins face the library, and the 10 m gap between them is the court's gate, spanned by their sky bridge and sky garden on the 26th floor. Each court has a playground, a chess pavilion and shade trees; a covered linkway runs round it from void deck to void deck; behind Block 9 the residents keep a community garden. The east precinct rings Estate Square with the neighbourhood centre and the hawker centre. The gaps are swing gaps: corridor rail to corridor rail with the Leaf Hat.

The Old Moy tower house

The kit for Old Moy, after the tower houses of old Sana'a: most stand on plumb ground storeys of dark stone, the oldest (about one in four, most of them round the first well) on a battered base of coursed rammed earth with rounded corners under a white gypsum cornice. Above either rise brick storeys banded at every floor in white gypsum friezes, every window outlined in white under a semicircular fanlight (the qamariya), box windows (the kishk) on brackets, and a crenellated parapet with whitewashed horns at the corners. The roof is a terrace: a kiosk where the ramp arrives, a wind tower turned to the west wind over it, and on some houses a rooftop room (the manzar). Inside, a switchback ramp core runs the full height and doubles as the house's air shaft. Each tower was built for one family; most are now a shop below and a flat on each floor above. Every Old Moy lot 6 m or more across takes the tower house; narrower lots hold the later tiled pencil infill.

The market lanes

Moy's first growth outside the well town, after Hong Kong's tenement shophouses (tong lau): a shop at the ground and flats above, shoulder to shoulder. The boom houses, four and five storeys, were built for the Windworks' first workers: rendered fronts, French windows with shutters, and on the main streets a veranda on square columns over the pavement, stacked floor on floor. The tile houses, six to nine storeys, came as the city grew: mosaic tile, balconies, a canopy over the shop, and on corners the rounded Streamline corner with balconies wrapping it. Narrow pairs share one ramp core on their party wall, the way tong lau pairs share a stair; slivers too narrow for a house hold two-storey lock-up shops. Arcade Street is roofed in the manner of a Tokyo shōtengai, and the night market fills Lamp Lane under strings of lanterns.

The library

A classic building renovated over the years, after Brooklyn's Central Library. Built in the same boom as the Grand Hotel, in art deco limestone and bronze, its front curves round Library Square like an open book, with a 15 m portal at the spine framed in gilded bronze figures from Moy's stories. Inside is the Great Hall, after Baltimore's George Peabody Library and Trinity College's Long Room: reading tables with green lamps on the floor, four tiers of cast-iron galleries round every wall, every wall shelved, every shelf reachable from its gallery, all under a louvred skylight. Ada's renovation opened the ground floor in glass behind the old stone, moved the children's library round a garden court, joined the Deck, and turned the old basement stacks into the heritage room, where the Great Hall's creaking floorboards are relaid and Nia keeps her drawer of maps nobody can explain. The hero climbs the hall through its iron: rail to rail through the gaps where the rolling ladders hang.

Five layers of movement

  • 1 · GroundLanes 3.2 to 4.8 m, shopping streets 6.4 to 8 m, main streets 9.6 to 12.8 m. No walk goes round more than about 60 m.
  • 2 · The DeckA 6 m walkway at +6.5 m. It rings the park over the park side of the Park Ring, a shaded arcade below and open street beside it. Branches run to the library and across Library Square, to city hall, to the hotel by skybridge, to the estate's roof garden and to the wet market's upper hall; BLOCK stays off it. Six ramp towers (R) and two glass lifts (L) lead down, so nowhere on it is more than 70 m from a way down.
  • 3 · Trains, +13.2 and +36 mNo track in Moy climbs. The Wall Line runs round the top of the wall (rail +13.2), stopping at the four gates. Two straight lines cross the city: the Spine Line north to south at +13.2 m down the middle of its streets, riding on the Deck's columns over the Deck and ending at a terminus just inside the wall at each gate, its platforms level with the Wall Line's a few steps away; and the Meridian Line east to west above it at +36 m, from the Meridian Arch (the East Gate) through the Timekeeper's empty centre over city hall to the West Gate's towers. They cross at the Crossing, the open station at the park's centre. Every platform's floor is the car's floor (rail + 1 m), so boarding is level. Escalators always come as an up and a down: pairs from the squares to the Deck, scissor stacks of pairs under the Spine's platforms and in the gate buildings, and at the Crossing four garden towers, each a double helix round a steel trunk with a glass lift beside it, from the lawn to the ring (+6.5), the Spine (+14.2) and the high line (+37). Every line runs a train every game hour on the same minute (every 60 real seconds). The Spine rides portal frames on the Deck's 12 m bay; the high line rides tree piers every 36 m, three Spine bays, set out from the Crossing, where the four towers carry both lines.
  • 4 · TowersLifts in every lobby. Downtown has sky lobbies; the estate's twin blocks share a sky garden on the 26th floor.
  • 5 · RoofsRoofs step by at most one storey, with 2 to 4 m gaps across lanes, so the hero can cross the city roof to roof.

Streets of 6 to 13 m between walls of 16 to 40 m are swing corridors for the Leaf Hat: the rope runs 12 to 52 m, and an anchor must be at least 7 m up.

The launch site

The way to the Space Kingdom. The ASAN launch vehicle, the ship from the Demo World, stands upright on a launch mount in the open desert north-east of Moy, about 440 m beyond the wall, between the Ice plateau and the main plateau's cliff. An unpaved track runs to it from the North Gate. It is built lying down in the hangar, rolled out on a strongback along the erector rails, and raised 90° about a hinge at the mount, after the Falcon 9's transporter-erector and the Soyuz rail erector, so the ship's nose points up and its doorway faces the launch tower's crew arm. Its two side boosters are strap-ons after the Falcon Heavy's: they separate in flight, relight, fly back and land upright on the two landing zones, so the idea reads on the ground, with folded legs and grid fins on the boosters, empty pads before a launch and boosters standing on them after.

  • BoardingThe track, the site gate, the tower lift to the crew arm, the doorway. The cabin deck stands vertical on the pad, so boarding is a seated launch.
  • LaunchCountdown, ignition of the four core bells and both boosters, lift-off and the roll, booster separation; then a fade to the Space Kingdom, where the ship flies level, as in the Demo World, without its boosters.
