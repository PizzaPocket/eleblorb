# Composition: facades, massing, roofs, dormers and the core vocabulary

This is the working vocabulary of architectural design. Use it to decide what a building should look like before you build it, and to name what you are building. Everything is expressed in Eleblorb's language: SuperEgg solids and superellipse openings (see section 1a of the main skill), with plain boxes only for floors, wall planes and seam fills.

## 1. Think in masses first

A building is never one box with parts stuck on. It is a **hierarchy of masses**.

- **Primary mass.** The largest, tallest volume, usually a rectangle with a gable roof. It carries the main ridge and the principal facade. Decide it first: footprint, storeys, ridge direction.
- **Secondary masses.** Smaller volumes that attach to it and clearly serve it: a **cross-wing** or **ell** (L, T, U or H plans), a **lean-to** (outshut) along the rear or flank, a **porch or portico**, a **tower or turret**, a **stair bay**, a **bay window**, an **annex** or **kitchen wing**, a **chimney breast**. Each secondary mass is lower, narrower or set back from the primary, so the eye reads one dominant volume with supporting ones.
- **Tertiary elements.** Dormers, canopies, steps, bay windows, shutters, brackets, chimney stacks, finials. They refine the masses and never compete with them.
- **Hierarchy rules.** One dominant mass per building. Secondary ridges sit at least 0.6 m below the primary ridge, or meet it as a cross-gable at the same pitch. Never two equal competing masses unless the plan is deliberately symmetrical (a hall with two matching wings). Step volumes down toward the garden, the yard and the back.
- **Plan shapes and what they say.** Rectangle: house, hall, barn. L: a house with a service wing or a corner shop. T: a hall with a cross-wing, a church-like nave and transept. U or courtyard: a farm, a palace, an inn with a yard. Compound: a family or craft community.
- **Composition balance.** Symmetrical (formal, civic, polite architecture): odd bays, central entrance, matching sides. Asymmetrical (picturesque, vernacular): balance a tall volume with a low long one, a heavy chimney mass with a wide window, a gable with a lean-to. Never leave a lopsided facade without an answer on the other side.
- **Entrance emphasis.** The way in is the focal point: centre it in symmetrical facades; mark it with a porch, portico, pediment, taller bay, pair of columns, steps, a lantern, a change of material. The more important the building, the more elaborate the entrance.

### The coordinated drawing set

Architecture is not a stack of independently generated storeys. Before making
geometry, keep these drawings in agreement:

1. **One plan per storey**, showing enclosing walls, partitions, structure,
   openings, ramps, service shafts, and intentional voids.
2. **At least one longitudinal and one cross section** through the important
   circulation or double-height space, showing floor thickness, ceilings,
   headroom, wall bearings, roof, and vertical services.
3. **A roof plan**, showing eaves, ridges, hips, valleys, changes of pitch,
   dormers, chimneys, drains, and the junction of every mass.
4. **Elevations**, checked against the same storey heights and opening locations.

The floor plate extends to the inner face of its enclosing walls and its
underside is the ceiling below. Any exception is a named, useful void. Upper
walls align with support below or sit on an explicit beam. The roof bearing line
comes from the top-storey walls; eaves are then offset from that line. Build
visual mesh and collision from these shared boundaries so a later room, ramp,
or roof helper cannot introduce a second, conflicting footprint.

## 2. Roofs: types, meanings and how to join them

Choose the roof for the climate, the span, the status and the culture; never by habit. Keep **one pitch per building** unless a deliberate secondary form (a lean-to, a tower cone) calls for a different one.

| Type | Form | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gable** | Two planes meeting at a ridge, triangular gable ends | Default for homes, barns, halls | 30 to 45 degrees; the gable end is the public face of a street house |
| **Hip** | Four planes meeting at ridge and hips | Formal houses, pavilions, windy sites, Chinese halls | No gable ends; low and stable; hips need a shorter ridge |
| **Half-hip or jerkinhead** | Gable with its peak clipped | Cottages, snowy or windy regions | Reduces wind uplift and snow load at the gable top |
| **Cross-gable** | Two gabled masses meeting at right angles | Cross-wings, T and L plans | Same pitch; the valley between them is where water gathers |
| **Gambrel** | Two pitches per side | Barns, needs a loft | Maximises loft room |
| **Mansard** | Steep lower slope, shallow upper | Urban, formal, adds a usable storey | Dormers pierce the steep slope |
| **Shed or lean-to (skillion)** | A single sloped plane | Porches, outshuts, workshops, canopies | Always falls away from the wall |
| **Catslide (saltbox)** | Main slope continues down over a rear lean-to | Cottages, extending a house cheaply | One long roof, two heights |
| **Pyramidal or hipped square** | Four planes to a point | Square towers, pavilions, granaries | Needs a finial |
| **Conical** | Round plan to a point | Turrets, mills, silos | Pairs with a drum |
| **Dome or onion** | Curved shell | Civic, religious, sky and fantasy cultures | A SuperEgg is a natural dome |
| **Flat with parapet** | Level roof behind a low wall | Arid and earth cultures, terrace dwellings | Needs scuppers; can be a walkable terrace |
| **Barrel or vault** | Curved over a rectangle | Warehouses, halls, ocean cultures | |
| **Tiered (pagoda)** | Stacked eaves, each smaller | Towers, gates, temples | Odd number of tiers |
| **Sawtooth, butterfly** | Angled or inverted planes | Workshops, modern or fantasy | Rare here |

**Roof adaptation between masses**
- **Parallel extension.** A lower wing continues in line with the primary ridge, its ridge lower, its eaves matching or stepped.
- **Cross-wing.** The secondary ridge runs perpendicular, meeting the primary slope with a **valley**; the cross-gable's ridge should meet the main roof below its ridge (or exactly at it only if the wing is as wide).
- **Lean-to.** One slope that starts below the main eave or continues it as a catslide; its pitch is shallower, never steeper.
- **Tower or turret.** A pyramidal or conical cap with a finial, set so its eave clears the main roof by at least 0.5 m.
- **Shared eave line.** Where possible align eave heights across masses; if they differ, differ clearly (by half a storey) so it reads as deliberate.
- **Overhangs.** Eaves overhang about one tenth of wall height (0.3 to 0.5 m here); wet climates and the Alps more; flat-roofed arid walls none.
- **Ridge.** Cap the ridge with a square-edged SuperEgg beam in the roof's colour; the two slabs are Boolean-cut to meet in one solid line.
- **Gable detail.** Bargeboards (vergeboards) on the rake, a gablet or louvre in the gable peak, a finial, return of the eave cornice.
- **Pitch by climate.** Snow and rain: steep (40 to 60 degrees). Moderate: 30 to 45. Dry: low or flat. Wind: hip or jerkinhead.
- **Never:** a tiny gable on a huge roof; a ridge that ends in the middle of nothing; a valley with no cross-wing under it; a flat roof where the culture uses pitched ones; pitches that differ by 5 degrees (differ by 10 or match).

## 3. Dormers

A dormer is a window in the roof slope that gives **light and headroom to an occupied attic room**. If there is no room under it, it is a lie.

- **Types.** Gabled (A-frame) dormer: the classic, with a small gable and cheek walls. Hipped dormer: a softer, windy-climate roof. Shed dormer: a wide, low-sloped box giving a whole bedroom headroom, usually across a third to a half of the roof's length. Eyebrow (swept) dormer: a curve flowing out of the roof plane, no cheek walls, picturesque and thatch-friendly. **Wall dormer (gablet or lucarne)**: the facade continues up through the eave into a gable, breaking the eave line, typical for a hay loft hoist or a showpiece bay. Louvred dormer: ventilation for a loft or granary.
- **Rules.** Align each dormer with a window or bay below it. Make it smaller than the windows below. Space them evenly, no wider than a third of the facade bay. Keep the dormer ridge at least 0.6 m below the main ridge. Same pitch as the main roof. Keep the dormer front back from the eave plane or flush with the wall below only if it is a wall dormer.
- **Where they belong.** Inns (sleeping wing), larger two-storey homes and home-workshops, the rescue hall. Small cabins and sheds do not get them.

## 4. Facade design

A good facade has **three horizontal layers** (base, middle, top) and a **vertical rhythm** of bays.

- **Base: plinth, water table, podium and pedestal.** The **plinth** is the thickened foundation band of stone (0.3 to 0.9 m) that lifts the wall off the ground and sheds water. A **podium** is a raised platform for a whole building (a hall on steps). A **pedestal** is the block under a column, post or statue: a base (plinth), a die, and a cap. Columns and porch posts stand on pedestals or stone bases, never directly in mud. The base is heavier and darker than the walls above it.
- **Middle: the wall, the openings and the order.** Walls carry the rhythm of windows and doors, **pilasters** (flat projecting piers) or **engaged columns** dividing the facade into bays, **quoins** (cornerstones) stacking at corners, **string courses** (horizontal bands) marking floor levels, and **pilasters or posts** carrying an **entablature**.
- **Top: entablature, cornice and roof.** The **entablature** is the horizontal band above columns: the **architrave** (lowest beam), **frieze** (middle band, often carved), **cornice** (projecting crown). On houses it reduces to a simple eave and fascia; on civic buildings it is bolder. **Corbels and brackets** support projecting eaves and balconies.
- **Pediment.** A low triangular gable (or segmental curve) over a portico, door or window that signals importance. Types: **triangular**, **segmental** (arched), **broken** (open at the apex), **swan-neck**. The triangular face is the **tympanum**, which can carry a carved symbol. Use a pediment for the civic hall and the inn's principal door; give homes a plain canopy.
- **Openings.**
  - **Proportion.** Windows are taller than wide (1:1.5 to 1:1.8) on vernacular and formal buildings; ground-floor windows are largest, upper ones progressively smaller; vertical alignment of openings from floor to floor.
  - **Surrounds.** An **architrave surround** frames the opening; a **lintel or keystone** carries the head; a **sill** and **apron** finish the base; **reveals** (the thickness of the wall) show depth; **hood moulds** shed rain; **shutters** fold back beside the opening; a **fanlight or transom** tops a door.
  - **Materials of the opening.** Frames, glazing bars (muntins), sills and lintels are made of the building's own local materials (oak timber, or stone on a stone storey), a shade darker than the wall so they blend; they are never painted in the accent colour. Glazing bars are slender. The one painted accent of an opening is the **shutters and door leaf**.
  - **Shutters follow purpose.** Shutters belong to dwellings and inns (privacy, weather, security for rooms people sleep and sit in). A workshop's working windows, a hall's leaded glass, a mill, a granary, a shed or a store get none (a store or shed gets no glazed window at all; ventilation is slatted boards). A workshop-home gets shutters on its living storey only. Each leaf is half the window's own shape (a superellipse), hinged at the window's edge and drawn open like a book, its straight meeting edge standing free of the wall; never a full-shape slab pasted beside the window.
  - **Rhythm.** Odd bays for symmetry (3, 5, 7). Solid to void about 3:1 on stone, 1:1 on glass-rich timber frames. Window spacing at least one window width. Never a wall of one window per cell.
  - **Door types.** Single, double, Dutch (split), cart door, wicket in a larger door; head: arched (superellipse) for entries that matter, flat lintel for service.
- **Corners and edges.** Quoins, corner posts, or a rounded masonry corner mark the edge. A building that meets the ground with no base reads as a stage flat.
- **Projections.** A **jetty** (upper storey overhanging on joists) in timber-frame cultures, a **bay or oriel** window (projecting glazed volume), a **balcony or gallery**, a **loggia** (recessed arcade), a **porch or portico** (covered entry), a **veranda** (deep covered platform), **buttresses** (masonry piers supporting a high wall), **steps and stoops**. Each projection casts shadow and builds depth; add one or two per facade, not ten.
- **Material and colour.** Two to three materials per building, heaviest at the bottom (stone, then plaster or timber, then roof), one accent colour for doors and shutters, trim darker or lighter than walls. The roof is the largest colour field; choose it with the setting.
- **Ornament.** Place it where structure ends: at the eaves, the gable peak, the door, the window head, the ridge. Make it from SuperEgg forms (rolled brackets, beaded rails, carved lintels) in the culture's motif. Never apply ornament in the middle of a blank wall.

## 5. Chimneys, hearths and fire

- A **stack** is a masonry mass rising through the building. Placement: gable-end external stack (the breast visible, stepped as it rises), central stack, paired end stacks, or a wall stack rising through a hip.
- It clears the ridge by 0.8 m or more and sits where the heat is needed: the hall, the kitchen, the forge or oven. The flue is **built into the wall**: the wall thickens at that point, and the rear of the stack shows on the outside as a **breast** with **offsets (weatherings)** stepping in toward a capped **flue crown**. A free-standing slab beside the wall is wrong.
- Fire safety: keep timber away from the hearth opening, use a stone hearth slab, and in workshops a stone floor and large mass.

## 6. Structure that shows

Let structure read in the design: tie beams across the hall, posts at porch corners, **trusses** (king post, queen post, hammer beam) in a great hall visible under an open roof, **brackets** under overhangs, **bay spacing** consistent with the structural module (here 3.2 m), **buttresses** where walls are tall and masonry. Anything that holds something up should look like it does and reach what it holds.

## 7. Proportion rules of thumb

- Storey height 3.25 m here; ground floor can run taller in civic buildings (4.5 to 6 m for a hall).
- Roof height (eave to ridge) 0.4 to 0.7 of wall height for a steep roof, 0.25 to 0.4 for a low one.
- Door height 2.4 m; door width 1.2 m; entrance porch width 1.5 to 2 times the door width.
- Window width 0.9 to 1.0 m (small), 1.6 to 1.8 m (large), height 1.3 to 1.5 m.
- Eaves overhang 0.3 to 0.5 m; plinth 0.3 to 0.9 m high; column height 8 to 10 times its width (square posts here).
- Chimney stack width one fifth to one quarter of the gable width.
- Roof ridge of secondary mass 0.6 m or more below the primary ridge.

## 8. Vernacular and polite

**Vernacular** buildings (homes, barns, workshops) grow from local materials and use: asymmetrical, practical, honestly structural, ornamented sparingly at doors and eaves. **Polite** (civic, noble, religious) buildings borrow a formal vocabulary: symmetry, columns, pediments, entablature, regular bays. In a village the hall, the inn, the mill-owner's house and the church are polite; cottages, sheds and barns are vernacular. Contrast between the two is what gives a settlement its hierarchy.

## 9. Applying it to a design (worked outline)

1. Primary mass: rectangle, ridge parallel to the street or green, 2 storeys.
2. Secondary: one cross-wing at the back (kitchen), one lean-to along the flank (store), one porch at the front.
3. Roofs: gable main, cross-gable wing at the same pitch with a valley, lean-to as a catslide.
4. Facade: stone plinth, plastered middle with a string course at the floor line, three bays (door in the centre, two windows each side above and below), quoins, a bracketed eave.
5. Chimney: gable-end breast on the cooler side, flue clearing the ridge.
6. Dormers: two gabled dormers aligned with the upper windows only if the loft is a room.
7. Check proportion, hierarchy, and that every projection earns its place.
