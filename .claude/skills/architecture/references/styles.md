# Real architectural styles: what makes each one itself

Each entry lists the signature features that make the style recognisable, what to leave out, and what it blends with. A settlement picks **one primary style**, and at most one secondary influence limited to named elements (see the Style Charter section of the main skill). Do not hybridise freely. Real regional architecture is cohesive because it uses a **small closed vocabulary** repeatedly; a convincing fictional kingdom does the same.

## European village traditions

### English medieval and Tudor vernacular (half-timbered)
- **Era and place.** 14th to 17th century, lowland England and the Welsh Marches.
- **Massing.** Hall houses, cross-wings, jettied upper floors, long ranges, lobby-entry in later houses.
- **Roof.** Steep gables (45 to 55 degrees), clay tile or thatch, half-hipped ends, deep eaves, tall chimneys of brick or stone on the gable or in the middle.
- **Walls.** Oak box frame with infill of wattle and daub or brick nogging, limewashed plaster, a stone or brick plinth, jettied upper floor with **dragon beam** at corners; close studding in prestigious fronts, square panels in plainer ones.
- **Openings.** Small leaded casements with mullions in horizontal runs, oriel bays, low doorways with four-centred arch.
- **Ornament.** Carved bargeboards, **pargeting** panels, brackets under jetties, carved door posts.
- **Palette.** Cream, ochre limewash, black-brown timbers, clay red roofs.
- **Leave out.** Classical columns and pediments, Georgian sash symmetry, Craftsman tapered posts.
- **Blends with.** Georgian simplification in later buildings (see below), Cotswold stone in stone regions.

### English Cotswold and stone vernacular
- Limestone walls (honey colour), steep stone-slab roofs at 50 to 55 degrees with stepped dormers (gablets), gables with coped ends and kneelers, mullioned windows with drip moulds, stone chimneys. Lean to half-timber only as a secondary influence in towns.

### Georgian vernacular (18th century)
- Symmetrical three- or five-bay fronts, **sash windows** in regular rows decreasing in height upward, a panelled door with **fanlight** and simple door case or portico, hipped or gable roof at 30 to 40 degrees, boxed cornice, brick in Flemish bond or rendered stone, chimneys paired at the gable ends. Polite architecture for halls, inns and mill-owners' houses. Do not mix with half-timbering on one building.

### Arts and Crafts and Craftsman (late 19th to early 20th century)
- **Purpose.** A reform movement celebrating honest hand-craft and natural materials, both in England (Arts and Crafts) and the United States (Craftsman, bungalow).
- **Massing.** Low, informal, horizontal; deep porches under the main roof; asymmetrical plans.
- **Roof.** Low-pitched gables (about 25 to 35 degrees) with **wide overhanging eaves**, **exposed rafter tails**, **knee braces** and **outriggers** under gables; often a broad front gable over the porch; shingle, tile, or thatch (English).
- **Walls.** Shingle, stucco or river-stone; **tapered square porch columns on stone piers** (the signature); built-in hearth of cobble or brick.
- **Openings.** **Bands of casements** or double-hung windows with a divided upper sash (three over one); wide, deep sills; heavy wooden doors with small windows.
- **Ornament.** Exposed joinery, pegs, brackets, simple geometric stained-glass, hand-hammered metal.
- **Palette.** Earth tones, forest green, ochre, brown, stone grey.
- **Leave out.** Fancy classical pediments, tall steep gables, Tudor half-timber unless the style is English Arts and Crafts.

### Dutch and Flemish
- **Stepped gables or curved (Dutch) gables** facing the street, narrow tall fronts, brick, tall windows, hoist beams in gables, shutters in two-tone paint.

### French Canadian (Quebec vernacular, "maison canadienne")
- **Massing.** Simple rectangular house, one and a half storeys, long, with the gable at the end; lean-to **gallery (galerie)** along the front or all round.
- **Roof.** **Steep gable (45 to 55 degrees) with a bell-cast (concave) flare at the eave** forming the veranda roof; metal (tin or copper) roofing, often red or silver; **dormers (lucarnes)** with small gables; end chimneys on the gable walls.
- **Walls.** Stone (fieldstone, whitewashed or left raw) or timber frame clad in clapboard; thick walls with deep reveals.
- **Openings.** Tall casements in small panes; **shutters in strong colour**; single door often on the long side under the gallery.
- **Ornament.** Simple turned gallery posts, scrollwork along the gallery eave.
- **Palette.** White walls, red or green roofs, coloured shutters.
- **Blends with.** Norman (the French ancestor), and in cold regions Nordic log at a modest level.

### Norman and northern French rural
- Steep roofs, half-timber with brick or flint, thatched roofs with curved eaves, **dovecotes**, large barns with stone gables.

### Alpine chalet (Swiss, Austrian, Tyrolean)
- **Massing.** Boxy house with a broad low-pitched **gable (15 to 25 degrees)** facing downhill, deep eaves 1.5 m or more, a **gallery (balcony)** around the upper floor, a stone ground storey (the stall) and a timber upper storey (the living floor).
- **Walls.** **Blockbau** (stacked log) upper floor with notched corners, board and batten in gable; stone or plastered base.
- **Ornament.** Carved balcony balustrades with cutout hearts or crosses, painted shutters, painted inscriptions and frescos, carved bargeboards.
- **Palette.** Dark weathered brown logs, whitewash, red shutters and flowers.
- **Leave out.** Steep Norse roofs.

### Scandinavian log and Norwegian vernacular
- **Massing.** Compact log cabins (**tømmerhus**), farm clusters arranged around a yard with separate buildings: **stabbur** (raised storehouse on stone piers with an overhanging gallery), barns, boat houses, a main house; **stave churches** with tiered shingled roofs and dragon-head finials.
- **Roof.** Steep gable (40 to 50 degrees) of **birch bark covered in turf**, wooden shingle or slate; turf roofs held at the eaves by **cut boards**; broad overhang.
- **Walls.** **Laft** log with scribe-fit or saddle notch, projecting corners, horizontal logs left raw or tarred black; sometimes **Falu red** (Sweden) or oxide painting; plank cladding in later buildings.
- **Openings.** Small windows with white frames, simple board doors, **carved door frames with rosemaling**.
- **Ornament.** **Rosemaling** (painted flowers), dragon-head and **vindskeid** (bargeboard) carvings on gables.
- **Palette.** Tar-black, oxide red, white trim, green turf.
- **Leave out.** Alpine balconies with heart cutouts if the style is Norse; Georgian symmetry.
- **Blends with.** Swedish red cottage, Finnish log, in the high north the Russian izba.

### Russian izba and northern log
- A cabin with an ornate carved **platband** around the windows (**nalichniki**), carved gable ends with a **pediment board and sun symbol**, a steep gable roof of boards or shingles, a stove in the middle. Compatible with Nordic log as a distinct ornament vocabulary.

### Mediterranean and Italianate rural
- Low-pitched clay tile roofs, thick limewashed masonry walls, small deep-set windows with shutters, loggias and arcades, rounded arches, shaded courtyards.

## East Asian traditions

### Chinese vernacular and imperial
See `cultures.md` and the glossary. Defining features: axial courtyard layout, odd number of bays, bracket sets (**dougong**), upturned eaves, ridge beasts, red columns on stone bases, tile colour by rank.

### Japanese minka and shrine architecture
- Thatched or tiled **irimoya (hip-and-gable)** roofs with deep eaves, post-and-beam frames with sliding paper doors (**shoji**) and verandas (**engawa**), plaster and timber walls, raised floors, torii gates.

## Earthen and arid traditions

### Pueblo and adobe
- Stepped, flat-roofed rooms of adobe or stone, projecting **vigas**, parapets with **canales**, roof access by ladder, **kivas** (round ceremonial rooms), domed **hornos** (ovens), thick battered walls, small deep windows.

### Middle Eastern and North African courtyard house
- Windowless street walls, an inner courtyard, **mashrabiya** lattice screens, wind catchers, domed roofs, thick mud brick or stone.

## Coastal and tropical traditions

### Stilt (pile) houses and over-water architecture
- Houses raised on piles, often on docks, steep thatched roofs at 45 to 60 degrees, open verandas, woven walls, rope lashings; in coastal Europe, **fishermen's huts** with tarred timber and net lofts.

### Tropical thatch and tree architecture
- Broad hipped or conical thatch roofs with deep overhangs, raised platforms, bamboo or timber poles, woven panels, open sides.

## Fantasy and invented traditions

Treat these as real traditions: derive them from environment, materials, belief and technology (see the Cultural Method in `cultures.md`), keep a small closed vocabulary, and repeat it. A fantasy style is "cohesive" when a viewer can list its five defining features and finds them on every building.

## Mixing styles without cacophony

- **Primary style 70 to 80 percent.** The massing, roof form and wall system come from one tradition.
- **One secondary influence, 15 to 20 percent.** Applied to specific, named elements only, such as a door case, a gallery, or a ridge ornament, and never to the roof form and wall system together.
- **One accent, up to 5 percent,** reserved for landmarks.
- **Compatible blends (shared ancestry):** English vernacular with Georgian; English with Arts and Crafts revival; Norman with French Canadian; Nordic with Alpine (log, steep), Nordic with Russian izba (carved ornament only); Pueblo with Moorish courtyard (arid). **Conflicts to avoid:** half-timber with Craftsman tapered columns; Georgian symmetry with Norse log; pagoda roofs with Georgian sash; Alpine heart cutouts with Chinese lattice.
- **Dialect rule.** If two influences must meet (a border town), put one on the **civic and polite buildings** and keep the vernacular buildings in the primary style.
